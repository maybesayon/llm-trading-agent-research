# 3. Data and Bias Controls

Every design choice in this section is motivated by a named threat to validity. We describe the data and universe (§3.1), the temporal-integrity procedures that address look-ahead bias and leakage (§3.2), the point-in-time procedures that address survivorship bias (§3.3), the protocol that measures training-data contamination (§3.4), and the validation steps — including a pilot study — that precede full-scale experimentation (§3.5). Dataset properties that can only be established empirically are marked as verification items and are confirmed during the pilot rather than assumed.

## 3.1 Data Sources and Universe

**Track A (historical, primary).** Our primary news and price source is the Financial News and Stock Price Integration Dataset (FNSPID), which combines 29.7 million stock-price records with 15.7 million time-aligned news records for 4,775 S&P 500 companies over 1999–2023, drawn from four financial news websites (Dong et al., 2024). We use FNSPID because it is, to our knowledge, the largest freely available corpus in which news and prices are already time-aligned, which reduces the alignment errors that arise when researchers join news and price data themselves. It carries three risks for our design. First, its news is drawn from four websites and therefore represents a sample of the news environment rather than its entirety; we treat our results as conditional on this information set and report per-ticker article counts so readers can assess coverage. Second, the density and timestamp granularity of its news coverage for our specific universe and window are dataset properties we have not yet measured; both are pilot verification items (§3.5), and the timestamp question is consequential enough that our execution rule is designed to remain conservative under the worst case (§3.2). Third, the dataset ends in 2023, inside the training window of any current LLM; this is not a disqualification but the central measurement problem of the study, addressed in §3.4. The Track A evaluation window is January 2022 through December 2023, chosen to include both a bear market (2022) and a recovery (2023) while remaining recent enough for adequate news density [verification item: to be confirmed in pilot].

**Track B (forward, secondary).** To obtain data that post-dates the pinned model's training cutoff, we assemble a six-month window (January–June 2026) with daily prices from Yahoo Finance and news from two free sources: the Finnhub company-news API (Finnhub, n.d.) and the GDELT project, which monitors global news media and provides timestamped, machine-readable records (Leetaru & Schrodt, 2013). Because GDELT is designed for event and tone monitoring rather than firm-level news retrieval, its precision when filtered to specific companies is a verification item distinct from raw coverage. Track B exists because no anonymization procedure can fully guarantee that a model's memorized knowledge has been neutralized; genuinely out-of-cutoff data is the only evaluation for which memorization is impossible by construction. Its cost is scale: the window is short, and the coverage and article quality of free news APIs for our universe is a verification item. Track B is therefore scoped as a validation check on Track A conclusions, not as an independent test with its own statistical power.

**Universe.** The universe is the twenty largest S&P 500 constituents by market capitalization as of December 31, 2021 — the last trading day before the Track A window opens — selected by rule and frozen before any experiment. Rule-based selection at the window's start reduces the risk of selection bias (choosing tickers on which a method is known to work) because the rule uses no information from the evaluation period. We acknowledge that mega-capitalization stocks are a deliberately conservative testbed: they are the most heavily covered and most liquid corner of the market, and the sentiment literature finds predictive content concentrated in stressed conditions rather than in such stocks generally (García, 2013). A null result here therefore does not preclude sentiment alpha elsewhere; the Limitations section returns to this point.

## 3.2 Temporal Integrity

The information set for any decision executed at the open of trading day *t+1* is restricted to data timestamped on or before the close of day *t*. Decisions are executed at the next session's opening price, never at the same session's close, because a close-of-day *t* execution would implicitly assume the strategy could trade on news published up to the instant of the close — the classic look-ahead error in daily-frequency backtests. This procedure reduces the risk of look-ahead bias; it cannot eliminate it entirely, because it depends on the accuracy of the source timestamps. Two safeguards address that dependency. First, the pipeline enforces the information-set rule mechanically: the backtest harness raises an error if any input record's timestamp postdates the decision time, rather than relying on correct upstream filtering. Second, if the pilot finds that FNSPID news timestamps are date-only rather than intraday [verification item], we adopt the conservative convention that an article dated day *t* may have appeared as late as that day's close, and is therefore first usable for the day *t+1* open decision — sacrificing signal freshness to protect integrity. For the supervised baseline (S4, §4), all model selection and hyperparameter tuning use only data preceding the evaluation window, so that no evaluation-period information leaks into training.

## 3.3 Survivorship and Point-in-Time Correctness

Survivorship bias is a documented source of spurious performance in LLM trading backtests (Jeon & Lee, 2026). It enters daily backtests through two doors: selecting the universe from firms known to have survived the evaluation period, and using price series that silently drop delisted firms. Our universe rule closes the first door by construction, since selection occurs before the window using only prior information. For the second, the practical exposure is limited — the twenty largest S&P 500 firms as of end-2021 — but not zero, and we handle it explicitly: if any universe member is delisted or acquired during the window, its position is liquidated at the last available price and the proceeds held at the risk-free rate, and the event is reported. Whether FNSPID and Yahoo Finance retain full price and news histories for any such firm, and whether Yahoo Finance's adjusted prices correctly reflect splits and dividends across our window, are verification items checked in the pilot against a second independent price source.

## 3.4 Memorization and Contamination Controls

Because the Track A window lies inside the training corpora of current LLMs, any LLM-based strategy's historical performance conflates genuine signal extraction with recall of memorized outcomes. Glasserman and Lin (2023) established the diagnosis and the core instrument: anonymizing company identifiers in news separates what a model infers from the text from what it retrieves about the named firm, and distinguishes look-ahead bias from the distraction effect of background knowledge. Jeon and Lee (2026) extended anonymization from sentiment scoring to agentic portfolio decisions, masking tickers and company names before their agents act. Our protocol inherits both elements: the anonymization instrument from Glasserman and Lin, and its application at the agent level from Jeon and Lee. It differs in purpose and scope in three respects. First, where prior work deploys anonymization primarily as mitigation — a way to obtain cleaner performance estimates — we deploy it as measurement: each LLM-based strategy (S5 and S6, §4) is run under three pre-specified conditions — original inputs, anonymized inputs, and anonymized inputs with shuffled dates — and the performance differences across conditions are themselves reported quantities, estimating how much of apparent performance memorization explains. Second, the protocol is applied uniformly across a graded strategy ladder rather than to a single system, allowing us to ask whether memorization effects grow with the model's degrees of freedom. Third, the historical estimate is cross-checked against Track B, where memorization is precluded by construction; agreement between the two tracks would support the protocol's adequacy, and disagreement would itself be an informative finding. We claim for this design a difference in emphasis and integration, not a wholly new instrument.

## 3.5 Validation Procedures and Pilot Study

Full-scale evaluation is preceded by a staged validation. First, the backtest harness is unit-tested: portfolio accounting identities (§4) are verified on synthetic price paths with known outcomes, and the temporal-integrity assertion of §3.2 is tested by deliberately injecting future-dated records and confirming the harness rejects them. Second, the passive and momentum baselines (S1, S2) are run over the full window and their outputs reconciled against an independent implementation of the same rules; disagreement beyond rounding indicates an accounting error, not a finding. Third, a pilot study — two tickers over a three-month sub-window — exercises the complete pipeline, including LLM calls, caching, and the anonymization conditions. The pilot evaluates pipeline integrity, data availability, and implementation correctness before large-scale evaluation; it is not an evaluation of strategy performance, and its trading outcomes will not be reported as evidence for or against any hypothesis. The pilot's specific verification targets are: FNSPID news density for the universe and window (assessed under the pre-specified criterion below); FNSPID timestamp granularity; delisted-firm and corporate-action handling in both price sources; Finnhub and GDELT coverage for Track B; and per-decision LLM cost and latency, which determine the feasibility envelope of the full run.

**News-density adequacy criterion (frozen before the pilot).** Adequacy is assessed by two metrics computable directly from the data. *M1 (availability):* the fraction of trading days in the window on which at least one timestamp-valid news observation for the ticker exists within the trailing three trading days — the same news window the text-based strategies consume — available before that day's close. *M2 (timestamp validity):* the fraction of a ticker's articles carrying a usable timestamp of at least daily granularity. The decision rule: a ticker passes if M1 ≥ 0.90 and M2 ≥ 0.95; tickers failing either bound are flagged as insufficiently covered and excluded from the primary analysis universe, with all exclusions reported; if more than five of the twenty universe tickers fail, Track A's news source is deemed inadequate for the design, triggering a documented protocol amendment (source augmentation or halt) before any strategy is run. The rationale: the M1 bound of 0.90 permits occasional quiet periods while ensuring that the information-flow assumption underlying daily text-based strategies — that decisions are informed by recent news — holds on nearly all decision days, and its trailing-window form ties the criterion to what the strategies actually read rather than to raw article counts, which vary with firm popularity; the M2 bound ensures the temporal-integrity rule of §3.2 is enforceable. Both thresholds are methodological choices frozen here, before the pilot, and are not revised in light of pilot or experimental outcomes. The criterion measures *availability of timestamp-valid information only*; it deliberately does not measure the informativeness or sentiment content of the news, so that low signal quality — however the strategies fare — is treated as a potential finding, never as grounds for excluding data. Pilot findings that alter the design (for example, a timestamp-granularity downgrade under §3.2) are documented as protocol amendments before the full experiment begins. For reproducibility, we record the exact version identifiers of all data sources — the FNSPID repository commit, the query dates and parameters for Yahoo Finance, Finnhub, and GDELT — and release the processing code and cached LLM outputs, so that the study's inputs can be reconstructed even where third-party data cannot be redistributed directly.
