# How Much of LLM Trading-Agent Performance Survives Bias-Controlled Evaluation? An Independent Study with a Rationale Audit

## 1. Introduction

Large language model (LLM) trading agents have produced some of the most striking performance claims in recent computational finance. FinMem, an agent equipped with layered memory and adjustable persona, reports leading trading performance relative to algorithmic baselines on real-world stock data (Yu et al., 2023). FinAgent, a multimodal successor, reports an average improvement of over 36% in profit against twelve state-of-the-art baselines across six financial datasets (Zhang et al., 2024). TradingAgents, which organizes LLMs into the specialized roles of a simulated trading firm, likewise reports notable improvements in cumulative return, Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown over baseline models (Xiao et al., 2024). Yet when Li et al. (2025) re-evaluated LLM-based investing strategies within a unified backtesting framework spanning two decades and more than one hundred symbols, the reported advantages deteriorated substantially: the strategies proved overly conservative in bull markets, underperforming passive benchmarks, and overly aggressive in bear markets, incurring heavy losses. The literature therefore contains two directly contradictory claims about the same class of systems.

This contradiction is less surprising than it first appears, because the evaluation conditions under which the two bodies of evidence were produced differ systematically. Quantitative finance has long documented that backtested performance is fragile: when many strategy configurations are trialed and only the best are reported, conventional performance statistics overstate true skill, a problem severe enough to motivate dedicated corrections such as the deflated Sharpe ratio (Bailey & López de Prado, 2014). LLM-based strategies inherit this fragility and add a further, technology-specific hazard. Although sentiment extracted by LLMs from news headlines has been shown to predict subsequent daily stock returns (Lopez-Lira & Tang, 2023), these models are trained on large web corpora that include historical financial news and commentary; a backtest conducted over a period contained in the model's training data is therefore contaminated in two ways: the model may possess direct knowledge of subsequent returns (look-ahead bias), and its background knowledge of specific companies may interfere with the measurement task itself, an effect Glasserman and Lin (2023) term distraction and find to be even larger than look-ahead bias. An evaluation that controls neither multiple testing nor memorization cannot adjudicate between the optimistic and skeptical readings of the evidence.

No existing study, to our knowledge, combines the elements such an adjudication requires. Developer-reported evaluations rely on generally short backtesting periods, are largely confined to US and Chinese equities, and rarely account for trading costs (Ding et al., 2024); moreover, their reported advantages deteriorate when the cross-section of assets is broadened and the evaluation horizon extended (Li et al., 2025). The corrective re-evaluation of Li et al. (2025) broadens the cross-section and horizon but does not quantify memorization effects, does not include a supervised machine-learning baseline operating on the same information set, and does not test on data generated after the evaluated models' training cutoffs. The present study contributes an evaluation designed around all four missing elements: a protocol frozen before any experiment is run, an explicit memorization-quantification procedure adapted from anonymization-based debiasing (Glasserman & Lin, 2023), a graded ladder of baselines that isolates where any added value originates, and a secondary out-of-cutoff validation window.

A second gap concerns not how these agents perform but how they explain themselves. LLM agents produce natural-language rationales for their trades, and these rationales are commonly presented as an interpretability benefit of the paradigm (Yu et al., 2023). However, research on chain-of-thought prompting demonstrates that model-generated explanations can systematically misrepresent the true basis of a model's prediction (Turpin et al., 2023). Whether the stated rationales of trading agents are consistent with their trading behavior — and whether that consistency holds across market regimes — remains unexamined in the trading domain.

We therefore ask two questions. RQ1: How much of the reported outperformance of sentiment-driven LLM trading agents survives bias-controlled evaluation — rule-based universe selection, cost-adjusted next-open execution, memorization checks, and multiple-testing correction — on US large-cap equities? RQ2: Are the agents' stated trading rationales consistent with their trading behavior, and how does this consistency vary across market regimes? Our working hypothesis is that reported outperformance is substantially attributable to evaluation artifacts and will not survive these controls. Both outcomes of RQ1 are informative: confirmation would establish that current evidence for LLM trading alpha is largely an evaluation artifact, whereas refutation would constitute the first bias-controlled demonstration of such alpha.

To answer these questions, we evaluate six strategies on an identical data universe of large-capitalization US equities: an equal-weight buy-and-hold benchmark, a momentum rule, a FinBERT sentiment rule, a gradient-boosted-tree model trained on the same sentiment and technical features, an LLM sentiment scorer, and a full LLM agent that issues decisions with stated rationales. Evaluation follows a two-track design: a historical track with explicit memorization quantification, and a forward, out-of-cutoff validation track. Section 3 details the data and bias controls; Section 4 specifies the strategies and the pre-specified statistical protocol.

This paper makes four contributions. First, we provide a pre-specified, bias-controlled comparative evaluation of sentiment-driven LLM trading strategies against passive, classical-quant, classical-NLP, and supervised-ML baselines on identical data. Second, we quantify the contribution of training-data memorization to historical LLM backtest performance using entity-anonymization and date-shuffling controls. Third, we introduce a decision-sensitivity audit — absent from prior work on trading agents — linking what these systems say to what they do across market regimes. Fourth, we release our code, cached model outputs, and evaluation harness to support reproduction and extension.
